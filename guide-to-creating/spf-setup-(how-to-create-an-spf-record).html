SPF Setup Explained: A Step-By-Step
Guide To Creating An SPF Record


The Sender Policy Framework (SPF) serves as a crucial protocol for email authentication, designed to safeguard domains against spoofing and unauthorized transmissions. By establishing an SPF record within your domain’s DNS settings, you specify the mail servers and services permitted to send emails on your behalf. Correctly configuring SPF enhances email security and assists receiving servers in verifying that messages originate from legitimate sources.


What Is an SPF Record?


An SPF record is a DNS TXT entry that specifies which servers and IP addresses are authorized to send emails on behalf of a domain. Upon receiving an email, the destination mail server verifies the sender against the domain's SPF guidelines. For instance, if your organization uses Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, or a third-party email marketing service, your SPF record should include these providers.

An SPF record typically looks like this:

v=spf1 include:example.com ~all

Here is what each component means:

  • v=spf1: Identifies the record as an SPF policy.

  • include.com: Authorizes the sending services specified by the referenced domain's SPF policy.

  • ~all: Marks other sending sources as softfail.

The actual record depends on your email providers and infrastructure.





Step-by-Step Guide to Creating an SPF Record


Step 1: Identify Your Email Sending Sources

Before setting up an SPF record, identify all services that use your domain to send emails, such as email platforms, marketing tools, CRM systems, and transactional email providers. Consult with your IT team or email admin to ensure all legitimate senders are included, as omitting any can lead to SPF failures and impact email delivery.


Step 2: Collect the Required SPF Information

Different email providers have distinct SPF requirements. Some necessitate the use of an include mechanism, while others assign specific sending IP addresses. For instance, a domain using Google Workspace might have the following SPF record:

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all

This should only be used if it aligns with your actual email configuration. Additionally, if your domain employs other sending services, their authorized sources should be included in the same SPF record.


Step 3: Create Your SPF Record

Consolidate necessary mechanisms into a single SPF policy. An example record could be:

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net ~all

This illustrates a domain utilizing both Google Workspace and SendGrid, assuming their SPF setups allow these entries. 

Note that a domain should typically contain only one SPF record; having multiple TXT records can lead to a PermError and disrupt email authentication.


Step 4: Access Your DNS Management Panel

Access the account managing your domain's DNS — this may be your registrar, hosting provider, or DNS service. Find the DNS management area and select the option to add a TXT record. Use "@" for the host if required. Insert your SPF policy into the TXT value field, then save your changes and confirm the record has been added correctly.


Step 5: Publish and Allow DNS Propagation

Once you have saved the record, it is important to allow sufficient time for DNS changes to take effect. The duration of this process varies based on your provider, caching settings, and the time-to-live (TTL) of the record. 

It is advisable not to create additional records during this period. Instead, utilize a DNS lookup tool to verify that the desired SPF policy is publicly accessible by checking the current TXT record.





Step 6: Test and Validate Your SPF Record

Utilize an SPF checker or DNS lookup tool to ensure your configuration is correct. This tool should verify the existence of the record, adherence to SPF syntax, and compliance with the DNS lookup limit.

According to RFC 7208, SPF evaluation is capped at ten DNS-querying terms. Surpassing this limit may result in a PermError.

When feasible, send a test email and review its authentication results. A passing SPF check is typically indicated as spf=pass in the headers of the received message.


Common SPF Setup Mistakes to Avoid


Configuration errors can undermine SPF protection and hinder the authentication of legitimate emails.

  • Creating multiple SPF records: Combine authorized sources into one SPF record.

  • Exceeding the DNS lookup limit: Review nested includes and unnecessary mechanisms.

  • Forgetting third-party senders: Include all legitimate services that send email for your domain.

  • Using incorrect syntax: Follow your providers' official configuration instructions.

  • Ignoring ongoing changes: Update your SPF policy when adding or removing email services.
