SPF Record Explained: Format, Syntax, And Best Practices

                               

An SPF (Sender Policy Framework) record serves as a DNS-based method for verifying email authenticity, allowing domain owners to designate which mail servers can send emails in their name. Familiarity with the structure, syntax, mechanisms, and recommended practices for SPF records is crucial for enhancing email security, minimizing spoofing, and ensuring dependable email delivery. When set up correctly, an SPF record complements DKIM and DMARC, forming a robust email verification system.


What Is an SPF Record?

An SPF record is set up as a TXT record within a domain's DNS configurations. It specifies which servers, services, or IP addresses are authorized to send emails on behalf of that domain. When a mail server receives an email, it can check the SPF record of the sender’s domain and verify if the sending server is among the approved sources.

This means the TXT record follows SPF version 1. The rest of the record includes mechanisms and qualifiers that delineate the valid senders and outline procedures for instances when a server is not recognized.

It's important to note that SPF does not encrypt emails or verify the identity of the visible sender. Rather, it aids receiving systems in determining if the sending setup is sanctioned by the domain's owner.



SPF Record Format

An SPF record has a specific format that includes a version identifier, methods of authorization, and potentially a conclusive policy directive.

In this case, v=spf1 denotes the version of SPF, ip4:192.0.2.10 grants permission to the given IPv4 address, and -all specifies that any other sending sources should not pass the SPF verification.

Companies often need to permit various email service providers. 

The specific record should accurately represent the services that are authorized to send emails on behalf of the domain.


Understanding SPF Syntax

The syntax of SPF employs various methods to designate permitted sending sources.

  • ip4: Grants permission to a specific IP address or range in IPv4 format.

  • ip6: Grants permission to an IPv6 address or range.

  • include: Allows sending services designated by another domain's SPF policy.

  • a: Permits IP addresses linked to the domain's A or AAAA records.

  • mx: Permits mail servers referenced in the domain's MX records.

  • exists: Conducts a DNS-based existence check.

  • all: Applies to all senders and is typically used at the end of an SPF record to determine the default outcome.

Qualifiers alter the assessment of a mechanism. A plus sign (+) indicates Pass, a minus (-) indicates Fail, a tilde (~) indicates SoftFail, and a question mark (?) signifies Neutral. The plus qualifier is usually assumed, making ip4:192.0.2.10 the same as +ip4:192.0.2.10.


SPF Policies: -all, ~all, and ?all

The ultimate mechanism, -all, carries significant importance. It results in an SPF Fail for any unauthorized senders and is typically employed when all legitimate sources for the domain have been thoroughly established.

On the other hand, ~all yields a SoftFail, which can be beneficial during a measured SPF rollout as organizations confirm their sending sources. However, it lacks the stringent enforcement implications of -all.

The ?all mechanism results in a Neutral outcome, offering minimal real protection since it fails to distinctly recognize unauthorized senders.


SPF Best Practices

Creating an SPF record is only the first step. Follow these best practices to maintain an effective configuration:


  • Publish only one SPF record: Multiple SPF TXT records for the same domain can cause authentication errors. Consolidate authorized sources into a single record.

  • Stay within the 10-DNS-lookup limit: SPF evaluations are limited to 10 DNS-query-causing mechanisms. Multiple include, a, mx, and similar mechanisms can quickly consume this limit.

  • Review third-party senders: Identify platforms such as Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, SendGrid, CRM systems, marketing platforms, support applications, and transactional email providers that send mail for your domain.

  • Use SPF flattening carefully: SPF flattening can replace DNS-based includes with IP addresses to help reduce DNS lookups. Services such as AutoSPF can help organizations monitor and manage changing sender infrastructure.

  • Test before enforcement: Validate the record after making DNS changes and confirm that legitimate sending services remain authorized.


SPF and DMARC Work Together

SPF is more effective when combined with DKIM and DMARC. SPF evaluates whether the sending server is authorized, while DKIM provides cryptographic authentication. DMARC adds domain alignment and allows domain owners to publish policies for messages that fail authentication.
